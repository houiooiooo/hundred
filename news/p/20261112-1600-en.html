Electronic musicScene

The dance floor in Ramallah

Ramallah is one of the main Palestinian cities in the West Bank, and the seat of the Palestinian government. In 2018 a bar there called Radio Bar threw a party, and about three hundred people danced as the sun went down. The British music streaming show Boiler Room broadcast it to the world — its first live stream from Palestine 1.

The DJ playing techno was Sama' Abdulhadi. Born in 1990, she was between ten and sixteen during the Second Intifada, the years when the Palestinian uprising against Israeli occupation and the fighting on both sides were at their worst. At eighteen she went to Beirut, Lebanon, to study sound engineering, heard techno in a club for the first time, and started playing. The video of that party has since been viewed more than ten million times.

She doesn't like being seen only as "the Palestinian DJ." She has said: don't book me because I'm Palestinian or because I'm a woman — book me for the music. But she also admits that politics follows her everywhere. In late 2020 she played at a historic site near Jericho. The organisers said they had a permit, yet the next day she was arrested by the Palestinian Authority and held for eight days before being released on bail. More than a hundred thousand people signed a petition for her release 2.

She isn't the only one. BLTNM is a label three friends started in Ramallah in 2016, making hip-hop and trap in Palestinian dialect. The band 47Soul joined the beat of dabke — the line dance people do at weddings and celebrations across the region — to electronic bass, and called it shamstep, Sham being the Arabic name for the region. In 2019 they played NPR's Tiny Desk in the US, and one of them broke into dabke between the office desks 3.

Sama' once explained why people at Palestinian parties go so hard: because it could always be the last one.

MainPrimer

Where is Palestine? Start with the map

The Palestine you hear about in the news is two separate pieces of land. One, in the east along the Jordan River, is the West Bank — a little larger than the US state of Delaware. The other, on the coast in the southwest, is the Gaza Strip: about twice the size of Washington, DC, and home to more than two million people. Israel lies between them 1.

The map didn't start out this way. It was built up over seventy-odd years by a few big events.

1948: Israel is founded. After the Second World War, Britain was pulling out, and in 1947 the UN proposed splitting the land into a Jewish state and an Arab state. When Israel declared independence, neighbouring Arab states sent in their armies and a war followed. About 700,000 Palestinians fled or were driven from their homes and were never allowed back. Palestinians call that year the Nakba, "the catastrophe."

1967: In another war, Israel occupied the West Bank and Gaza. To this day most of the world regards the West Bank as occupied territory.

The 1990s: The two sides signed the Oslo Accords, meant as a step-by-step road to peace. The West Bank was divided into three kinds of area: some run by Palestinians, some shared, and more than 60 percent controlled by Israel, known as Area C. The peace process never finished, and this "temporary" division is still in place. The villages in this issue's news story are in Area C.

From 2007: The armed group Hamas took control of Gaza, and Israel put Gaza under blockade, restricting people and goods coming in and out.

7 October 2023: Hamas attacked southern Israel; Israel says more than 1,200 people were killed and 251 taken hostage. Israel went to war in Gaza. According to Gaza's Ministry of Health, more than 74,000 people had been killed as of 5 November 2026. A ceasefire began in October 2025, but Israel still controls about 60 percent of Gaza 2.

Background｜Who is saying "genocide"?

You may have seen the word "genocide" in the news or on placards. In this war it is used in three different ways, and it helps to keep them apart:

・The International Court of Justice (the UN's court): South Africa brought a case against Israel. In 2024 the court told Israel to prevent genocide and let humanitarian aid in, but it has not ruled on whether genocide has happened; the case is ongoing.
・The UN Commission of Inquiry: in 2025 it concluded that Israel had committed genocide in Gaza, and in 2026 it said this continued after the ceasefire.
・The Israeli government: rejects this entirely and says it is fighting a war of self-defence against Hamas 3.

So when you read news about Palestine, ask two things first: which piece of land, and who is speaking?

NewsWorth knowing

Shepherd villages in the southern West Bank: after the demolitions

The primer mentions Area C of the West Bank. This story happens there. In the hills of the southern West Bank is an area called Masafer Yatta, where about a thousand people live scattered across a dozen or so small villages. Most raise sheep and goats; some families live in caves.

In the early 1980s Israel declared the area a military firing zone for training troops. From then on, the people living there were treated as illegal builders: houses, water connections and animal pens could all be torn down. In 2022 Israel's Supreme Court ruled against the residents, finding they could not prove they had lived there before the zone was declared, and allowed the army to remove about a thousand people 1.

On Wednesday 2 September this year, the army demolished every house in one of the villages, al-Taban. A human rights group counted 12 families, about 70 people, left homeless; the UN counted 4 households of 23 people, plus 6 households of seasonal residents affected — the two count differently. The Israeli side says it was removing illegal buildings inside a training zone 2.

What this story wants to dwell on is how people there respond:

・Filming it: villager Basel Adra has spent years filming his village being torn down. No Other Land, the documentary he made with the Israeli journalist Yuval Abraham and others, won the 2025 Oscar for best documentary.
・Taking evidence to court: in July 2025 the local activist Awdah Hathaleen was shot dead by an Israeli settler, and it was caught on video. In August this year prosecutors charged the man who fired. A human rights group says the last time an Israeli civilian was charged for killing a Palestinian was in 2019. The defence says it will fight the charges 3.
・Staying and rebuilding: in 2017 local young people formed Youth of Sumud — sumud is Arabic for holding on, refusing to leave. They restored Sarura, a cave village emptied in 1998, and walk children to school and shepherds to their grazing land. After al-Taban was demolished, people from neighbouring villages came to clear the rubble and rebuild the pens.
・Being there: Israeli and international volunteers take turns staying in the villages, hoping that outsiders' presence means fewer attacks. Hamdan Ballal, one of the film's directors, says volunteers can't protect everyone, but villagers feel a little safer.

TattooTradition

Tattoo stamps in Jerusalem, and the stitches of Palestinian women

Getting a tattoo as a souvenir of a pilgrimage to the Holy Land is a custom at least three hundred years old in Jerusalem. Around Easter 1697, an English chaplain described what he saw: the artist pressed a wooden stamp carved with a design, dusted with charcoal, onto the arm, then pricked along the lines with two fine needles. The ink, he was told, was gunpowder mixed with ox gall, and the finished tattoo was washed with wine 1.

The craft is still alive. In Jerusalem's Old City there is a tattoo shop run by the Razzouk family. They say their ancestors were in Egypt, tattooing small crosses on the wrists of local Christians — the mark that let them into church — and brought the craft to Jerusalem about five hundred years ago. Today's owner, Wassim, says he is the 27th generation and grew up watching his father and an aunt tattoo. The family's old stamps were once recorded by a researcher: 168 of them, one carved with the year 1749 2.

Stamps let a design be copied unchanged, generation after generation. Palestinian women passed on patterns another way: embroidery, called tatreez in Arabic. The patterns weren't drawn on paper; they were kept in the hands, taught by mothers to daughters. You used to be able to tell where a woman came from by her dress: Ramallah dresses had palms embroidered on the back, dresses from coastal Jaffa had cypresses, and the colours and motifs also showed whether she was married and how well off her family was. UNESCO added it to its intangible heritage list in 2021. Widad Kawar, who collects these dresses, has said: "Every region has a motif, and every motif has a story." And: "A soldier can take away a flag, but not a woman's dress." 3

Some people now carry these memories in their skin. The Palestinian artist Leila Abdelrazaq had poppies and seeds tattooed on her hand. She writes that these images are her own memories and also symbols any Palestinian would recognise.

ThreadCounterpoint

Does boycotting Israel work?

The position challenged: boycott (BDS) can force Israel to change its policies

Supporters of Palestine often call for a boycott of Israel. The movement is known as BDS — Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions: don't buy Israeli products, don't invest in companies involved, and push governments to impose sanctions. At this May's Eurovision Song Contest, broadcasters in five countries — Spain, Ireland, the Netherlands, Iceland and Slovenia — boycotted because Israel was taking part. Israel took part anyway 1.

The sceptics ask: has any of this actually changed anything?

Their first argument is that boycotts can make Israelis dig in. In 2015 the EU required products from settlements to be labelled as such. Researchers who surveyed Israeli opinion afterwards found people became more supportive of hard-line policies. Only in one extreme and unlikely situation were people more willing to compromise: a full boycott by their most important ally 2.

The second argument is economic. Much of what Israel sells abroad is hard for buyers to replace, or is parts and components that shoppers never see in a supermarket — unlike South Africa back then, which mostly sold minerals and farm produce. So researchers argue consumer boycotts make little difference; only sanctions by the US or EU governments would create real pressure.

Supporters reply that boycotts were never just about consumers. They point to South Africa: scholars of the sanctions against apartheid argue that financial sanctions, oil and arms embargoes, plus isolation in sport and culture, together weakened the regime. On this view, boycotts work by changing what people see as acceptable, until states and big institutions have to act. There are recent examples: in August 2025 Norway's state fund — the world's largest sovereign wealth fund — sold its shares in Caterpillar, the bulldozer maker, and five Israeli banks, citing the demolition of Palestinian homes and the financing of settlements; in October that year, Spain's parliament passed an arms embargo on Israel 3.

The sceptics answer that these gains can be reversed. That November, Norway's parliament called for a review of the fund's ethical rules, and the government paused new exclusions (shares already sold were unaffected). An EU proposal to suspend the trade terms of its agreement with Israel failed this April for lack of support.

So the argument comes down to one question: can this pressure build, bit by bit, until the US or EU governments act? So far it hasn't. And even if they did, whether that would change Israeli government policy is still an open question.

LiteraturePoet

Darwish: the Palestinian poet who didn't want to be only a "poet of resistance"

If you had to name one figure almost every Palestinian knows, it would probably be the poet Mahmoud Darwish. His poems have been set to music and sung, and his most quoted line is: on this earth, there is what makes life worth living.

He was born in 1941 in a village in the Galilee. In the 1948 war his family fled to Lebanon. The next year he came back with his grandfather and found the village razed, with Israeli farms and a new settlement built in its place. Because they had been away during the war, they had no papers and were classed as "present absentees": physically there, legally nonexistent 1.

He later lived in Haifa, worked as a newspaper editor, wrote poetry, and was arrested and placed under house arrest by the Israeli authorities several times. His 1964 poem "Identity Card" made his name; in it, the speaker tells the registering official: write it down, I am an Arab. In the early 1970s he left Israel and spent more than twenty years in exile between Cairo, Beirut and Paris, and in 1988 he helped draft the Palestinian Declaration of Independence.

People called him the "poet of resistance," but he was wary of the label. He said that forcing poetry to serve the nation did no good for poetry or for Palestine, and he worried readers would read him only through politics 2. He wanted his poems read as poems.

In 2000 something unusual happened. Israel's education minister, Yossi Sarid, from a left-wing party that favoured reconciliation, wanted to put four of Darwish's poems on the high school reading list — one of them "To My Mother." The right was furious and tabled a no-confidence motion in parliament, and Prime Minister Barak said the time wasn't right. Darwish said, with some irony: "Perhaps Barak is right." He believed peace had to come before people could read each other's literature; and he criticised Israeli society for not yet being ready to see Palestinians as poets and creators, rather than only as a political problem 3.

He returned to Ramallah in the 1990s, died in 2008 after heart surgery in the US, and is buried in Ramallah, where a museum now stands beside his grave.
