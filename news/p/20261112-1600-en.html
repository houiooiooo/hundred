Electronic musicScene

The dance floor in Ramallah

In 2018, at a bar in Ramallah called Radio Bar, about three hundred people danced as the sun went down. It was Boiler Room's first live-streamed party in Palestine, and the DJ playing techno was Sama' Abdulhadi 1.

That video has since been reported to pass ten million views. Born in 1990, she lived through the Second Intifada between the ages of ten and sixteen, and at eighteen, while studying sound engineering in Beirut, she heard techno in a club for the first time. She has said: "I don't work as a Palestinian, I'm a techno DJ." And: "Don't book me because I'm Palestinian or because I'm a girl — book me for the music." In the same interview she also said: "Politics follows me everywhere."

In late December 2020 she played at the Nabi Musa site near Jericho. The organisers said they had a permit and that the venue was a market area separate from the shrine. The next day she was arrested by the Palestinian Authority and held for eight days before being released on bail; a petition for her release gathered more than a hundred thousand signatures 2.

The same city has other sounds. BLTNM is a label started in Ramallah in 2016 by three friends, making hip-hop and trap in the local dialect and moving lately toward bass and synths; the rapper Shabjdeed says "we are the new Palestine." The members of 47Soul met in Amman and joined the beat of dabke, the Levantine folk dance, to dubstep, calling it shamstep — Sham being the Levant. In 2019 they played NPR's Tiny Desk, and member Walaa Sbait broke into dabke between the office desks 3.

Sama' once explained why Palestinian parties go so hard: "It could always be the last one."

TattooTradition

Wooden stamps in Jerusalem, and stitches on a dress

In March 1697, around Easter, the English chaplain Henry Maundrell watched pilgrims being tattooed in Jerusalem. He noted that the artists kept wooden stamps carved with different designs; they dusted a stamp with charcoal and pressed it on the arm, then pricked along the lines with two fine needles dipped in ink — made, he was told, from gunpowder and ox gall — and washed the work with wine 1.

The practice is still alive. The Razzouk family in the Old City say their ancestors tattooed small crosses on the wrists of Coptic Christians in Egypt so they could enter churches, and brought the craft to the Holy Land about five hundred years ago; today's Wassim Razzouk says he is the 27th generation. The family's olive-wood stamps were once recorded by the researcher John Carswell, 168 in all, one of them carved with the year 1749. That is the count of the historical collection, not necessarily the number still kept in the shop. Wassim says he grew up watching his father and an aunt tattoo 2.

Stamps let a design be copied unchanged from one generation to the next. Palestinian women's embroidery, tatreez, travels another way: the patterns are kept in the hands and passed from mother to daughter. UNESCO added it to its intangible heritage list in 2021, noting that colours and motifs show which region a woman comes from, as well as her marital and economic status. Dresses from Ramallah carry palms on the back; those from coastal Jaffa carry cypresses. The collector Widad Kawar has said: "Every region has a motif, and every motif has a story." And: "A soldier can take away a flag, but not a woman's dress." 3

The Palestinian artist Leila Abdelrazaq, in "On Tattoos, Talismans, and the Sacred Palestinian Body," writes about working with the tattoo artist Haitham Haddad to tattoo poppies and seeds on her hand: images that are private memory and, at the same time, symbols Palestinians recognise together. She treats the body as a place that carries collective memory.

MainPrimer

Where is Palestine? One map, seventy-odd years of layers

The Palestine people talk about today is two separate pieces on the map: the West Bank in the east, along the Jordan River, and the Gaza Strip in the southwest, on the sea, with Israel in between. East Jerusalem lies on the West Bank side.

The map was built up from a series of ruptures.

In 1947 the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 181, dividing British Mandate Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state, with Jerusalem under international administration. Israel was founded the following year, and in the war about 700,000 Palestinians fled or were driven from their homes. Palestinians call it the Nakba, "the catastrophe" 1.

In the 1967 war Israel occupied the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem, and later annexed East Jerusalem. The Oslo Accords from 1993 were meant as an interim arrangement: the West Bank was cut into Areas A, B and C, with Area C — more than 60 percent of the West Bank — under near-total Israeli control. Masafer Yatta, in this issue's news story, is in Area C.

In 2007 Hamas took control of Gaza by force, and Israel then blockaded it. On 7 October 2023 Hamas attacked southern Israel; by Israel's count more than 1,200 people were killed and 251 taken hostage. Israel went to war in Gaza. According to Gaza's Ministry of Health, 74,618 people had been killed as of 5 November 2026. Since the ceasefire of October 2025, Israel still controls about 60 percent of Gaza 2.

The word "genocide" has appeared in this war in different legal proceedings, investigative findings and political responses. South Africa brought a case against Israel at the International Court of Justice under the Genocide Convention. On 26 January 2024 the court ordered Israel to take all measures within its power to prevent acts listed in Article II of the Convention, and to take immediate, effective measures to enable urgently needed basic services and humanitarian aid; on 28 March it further ordered Israel to ensure unhindered aid at scale. These were provisional measures: the court did not order a full ceasefire and has not ruled on the merits of whether genocide has occurred. The UN Human Rights Council's Commission of Inquiry concluded in September 2025 that Israel had committed genocide in Gaza, and its October 2026 report found that acts with genocidal intent continued after the 2025 ceasefire. The Israeli government rejects this entirely; its foreign ministry called the 2025 report "distorted and false" and describes the war as self-defence against Hamas 3. The West Bank is a separate thread: the ICJ's advisory opinion of July 2024 found the occupation unlawful, which the Israeli prime minister's office called a "decision of lies."

When you read news about Palestine, two questions help first: which piece of land, and who is speaking.

When a court, a UN commission and a government use different words for the same war, how do you decide whom to believe?

NewsWorth knowing

Masafer Yatta: homes torn down — cameras, courts and rebuilding

On Wednesday 2 September, the Israeli military demolished the houses of an entire village, al-Taban, in Masafer Yatta in the southern West Bank. The Guardian and Al Jazeera reported 12 families, about 70 people, left homeless; the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) counted 27 structures, 4 households of 23 people displaced, and 6 more households of seasonal residents affected. COGAT, a unit of Israel's defence ministry, called it an "enforcement operation" against illegal construction in a military training zone 1.

About a thousand people live scattered across a dozen or so hamlets here, most of them herders. In the early 1980s Israel declared the area Firing Zone 918. In May 2022 Israel's Supreme Court rejected the residents' petition, finding they could not prove they had lived there permanently before the zone was declared, and ruled that the state could remove about a thousand people for military training. OCHA recorded another 122 people displaced inside the firing zone between mid-August and late September this year 2.

The response starts with recording. Basel Adra, a volunteer with the human rights group B'Tselem, filmed his village being demolished; No Other Land, which he made with the Israeli journalist Yuval Abraham and others, won the 2025 Oscar for best documentary.

Recording also reached a courtroom. In July 2025 the activist Awdah Hathaleen was shot dead in the village of Umm al-Kheir, and video showed the settler Yinon Levi firing. On 6 August this year prosecutors indicted him on charges including reckless manslaughter. The rights group Yesh Din says the last time an Israeli civilian was indicted for killing a Palestinian was in 2019; B'Tselem called it "the exception that proves the rule." Levi's lawyer says he will contest the charges 3.

In the villages, young people from At-Tuwani formed Youth of Sumud in 2017, restoring the cave hamlet of Sarura, emptied in 1998, and accompanying children to school and shepherds to their grazing land. Israeli and international volunteers take turns sleeping in the villages, but volunteers were present when Hathaleen was killed; one of the film's directors, Hamdan Ballal, says volunteers can't protect Palestinians, "but they make them feel a little safer." After al-Taban was demolished, people from neighbouring villages came to clear the rubble and rebuild the sheep pens.

ThreadCounterpoint

Does boycott work?

The position challenged: boycott (BDS) can force Israel to change its policies

The Eurovision Song Contest of May 2026 was held in Vienna. Public broadcasters in five countries — Spain, Ireland, the Netherlands, Iceland and Slovenia — boycotted it over Israel's participation. Israel took part as usual 1.

The call to Boycott, Divest and Sanction (BDS) keeps getting louder. The other side asks: what has it changed?

The case against begins with public opinion research. The political scientists Guy Grossman, Devorah Manekin and Yotam Margalit studied how Israeli opinion shifted after the EU required settlement products to be labelled in 2015. The result was backlash: more people backed hard-line policies. Their survey experiments also found that Israelis supported concessions only under one extreme, unlikely scenario: a full boycott by a country seen as a key ally 2.

Economic analysis points the same way. Dany Bahar and Natan Sachs of the Brookings Institution noted that, on 2015 trade data, about half of Israel's exports by value were differentiated goods that are hard to replace, and about 40 percent were intermediate goods consumers rarely see; South Africa's exports back then were more than 60 percent commodities. They concluded that only official sanctions by the US or the EU would create serious pressure.

Supporters answer that consumer boycotts were never the point. They cite South Africa: Neta Crawford and Audie Klotz, who studied sanctions against apartheid, argue that financial sanctions, oil and arms embargoes, and cultural and sporting isolation weakened the regime in direct and indirect ways. On this view, boycott works by shifting norms and moving states and large institutions. There are recent examples: in August 2025 Norway's sovereign wealth fund sold its holdings in Caterpillar and five Israeli banks, citing the demolition of Palestinian property and the financing of settlements; in October that year Spain's parliament passed an arms embargo on Israel 3.

The other side responds: these gains can be reversed. In November 2025 Norway's parliament called for a review of the fund's ethical framework, and the government then introduced temporary guidelines halting new exclusion recommendations by the ethics council and new exclusion decisions by the central bank; existing exclusions were not automatically lifted. An EU proposal to suspend the trade terms of its agreement with Israel failed in April 2026 for lack of support.

The dispute narrows to an empirical question: can this pressure build until the US or the EU imposes official sanctions? Grossman and his colleagues found that, in their survey experiments, a full boycott led by a key strategic ally was what made respondents more likely to support policy concessions. But opinion experiments cannot directly prove whether boycotts or sanctions change government policy; how boycott turns into institutional pressure remains an open question.

LiteraturePoet

Darwish, who didn't want to be only "the poet of resistance"

In 1949 Mahmoud Darwish returned with his grandfather from Lebanon to the Galilee. His home village, al-Birwa, had been razed, and a collective farm and new settlement built in its place. The family had no papers and were classed as "present absentees": physically there, legally nonexistent 1.

He later lived in Haifa, joined the Israeli Communist Party and edited its newspaper, and was arrested and placed under house arrest several times. In 1964 he wrote "Identity Card," in which the speaker tells the registering official: write it down, I am an Arab. In the early 1970s he left and lived in exile between Cairo, Beirut and Paris, and in 1988 he helped draft the Palestinian Declaration of Independence.

The title "poet of resistance," a review in The Nation said, followed him around like a Homeric epithet. He was wary of it. He said that clinging to poetry as a servant of the national cause was "useless, serving neither poetry nor Palestine"; he feared becoming "once again a prisoner of political reading" 2. He wanted his poems read as poems, and he was unhappy with the more direct resistance poems of his Beirut years.

In 2000 Israel's education minister Yossi Sarid proposed adding four of his poems, including "To My Mother," to the high school curriculum. Prime Minister Barak said the time was not yet ripe, and right-wing parties tabled a no-confidence motion. Darwish said, with irony at the time: "Perhaps Barak is right." He thought peace should come before cultural exchange; but he also criticised Israeli society for not yet being ready to recognise Palestinians as creators of culture rather than only as a political problem 3.

His most quoted line comes from "On This Earth": on this earth, there is what makes life worth living.

In his last years he returned to Ramallah. He died on 9 August 2008 after heart surgery in Houston, and is buried beside Ramallah's Cultural Palace, now home to his museum.
